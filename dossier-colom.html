Scott Colom

The Blue Slip. District attorney of Mississippi’s 16th Judicial District since 2016, blocked from the federal bench in 2023 without a hearing — and now United States senator-elect from Mississippi. Compiled from open sources only.

BACKGROUND / COVER STORY

Subject is a seventh-generation Mississippian, born and raised in Columbus, and the law is the family trade on both sides. His father, Wilbur O. Colom, is a Columbus attorney who was the Republican nominee for Mississippi state treasurer in 1987. His mother, Dorothy Winston Colom, was the first Black person elected to a chancery court judgeship in their district.

No cover needed: subject's own career is a staircase of firsts through the courthouses of northeast Mississippi — a judgeship at twenty-eight, a prosecutor's office taken from a thirty-year incumbent — followed by a federal judgeship that died without a hearing on a single senator's withheld blue slip. The last entry in the file is what he did about that.

SERVICE RECORD (CAREER FILE)

  • Education and early years. Columbus High School; Millsaps College, B.A. in English and history, 2005; University of Wisconsin Law School, J.D. cum laude, 2009. Between college and law school he spent a year teaching in Guyana. After law school he worked as a staff attorney at the Mississippi Center for Justice (2009–2011) and ran The Colom Law Firm (2011–2016) alongside part-time public posts.
  • 2011 — The bench, first. Appointed a justice court judge in Lowndes County at twenty-eight: the youngest ever appointed to that bench, and its first Black judge. He also served as a municipal court judge in Aberdeen (2012–2013).
  • 2012 — The rap-song case. As a private attorney he represented Taylor Bell, an Itawamba high school student disciplined for an off-campus rap song criticizing two coaches. A Fifth Circuit panel ruled for Bell on First Amendment grounds; the en banc court ultimately upheld the school (Bell v. Itawamba County School Board).
  • 2013–2016 — The prosecutor, first. Part-time city prosecutor of Columbus: the city's first Black city prosecutor.
  • 2015 — The thirty-year incumbent falls. Elected district attorney for the 16th Judicial District (Clay, Lowndes, Noxubee, and Oktibbeha counties), defeating Forrest Allgood, the incumbent of nearly thirty years. A political action committee funded by George Soros, Mississippi Safety & Justice, spent heavily in support of his race — reported at $926,500. That fact follows this file; see 2023. Sworn in January 4, 2016: the first Black district attorney of the district, and the first Black person ever elected district attorney in a majority-white voting district in Mississippi. Re-elected unopposed in 2019; re-elected in 2023 with over fifty-six percent.
  • 2017 — The reformer's profile. The New York Times profiled him among "5 Prosecutors With a Fresh Approach." He expanded pretrial diversion and is credited with setting up Mississippi's first restorative justice program. His office's own reporting claims a conviction rate above ninety-five percent and twenty-seven violent offenders prosecuted by subject personally — self-reported figures; see the File Note.
  • 2021 — The exoneration. Through his office's efforts, Eddie Lee Howard, who had spent twenty-three years on death row for a murder conviction built on discredited bite-mark evidence, was exonerated and the charge dismissed.
  • October 14, 2022 — The nomination. President Biden announced his intent to nominate subject to the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Mississippi, to the seat vacated by Judge Michael P. Mills; the nomination was sent to the Senate on November 15, 2022, on the recommendation of Congressman Bennie Thompson. Support crossed party lines: Senator Roger Wicker returned his blue slip, and former Republican governors Phil Bryant and Haley Barbour backed the nomination. Confirmed, subject would reportedly have been only the second Black judge ever to serve on that court.
  • April 4, 2023 — THE BLUE SLIP. Senator Cindy Hyde-Smith announced she would not return her blue slip, which under Senate tradition ended the nomination without a hearing or a vote. Her stated grounds: that subject supported transgender athletes in girls' and women's sports, and the Soros-funded PAC's support in 2015. Subject answered on the record in an April 10, 2023 letter asking her to reconsider: he had never requested the PAC's money, did not know it would be spent, received none of it when re-elected in 2019, and never discussed any policy or decision with the PAC or with Soros — and he had never taken a position on transgender sports; what he had signed, with other prosecutors, was a 2021 letter condemning the criminalization of transgender people and gender-affirming care. The nomination, returned once in January 2023 and renominated, was returned to the president for the last time on January 3, 2024.
  • September 2025 — He runs for her seat. Subject announced for the United States Senate, against Hyde-Smith, pitching cost of living and corruption; he said her vote for the 2025 tax-and-spending law, with its Medicaid cuts, spurred the run. Endorsed by Barack Obama; Kamala Harris rallied for him at home in Columbus on October 22, 2026.
  • March 10, 2026 — The primary. Won the Democratic nomination over Priscilla Williams-Till and Albert Littell.
  • 2026 — "Pick another senator." The race's signature moment was built from the incumbent's own words: asked in February 2026 about the rising cost of beef, Hyde-Smith said Mississippians "have so many proteins to choose from." Subject's campaign cut the viral answer: Mississippians saying they would rather pick another senator. Polling aggregators gave subject less than a ten percent chance of winning.
  • November 3, 2026 — Election Day. Subject against Hyde-Smith and independent Ty Pinkins, the previous Democratic nominee.
  • November 11, 2026 — The call. Eight days after Election Day, CNN and the Associated Press called the race for subject, by roughly half a percentage point — about four thousand four hundred votes at the call. Hyde-Smith conceded.

CURRENT STATUS — THE SENATOR-ELECT

Subject is the United States senator-elect from Mississippi, succeeding the senator who blocked his judgeship, and still serving as district attorney of the 16th Judicial District until he takes the Senate seat. On the public record he will be the first Black senator from Mississippi since Blanche K. Bruce, who left office in 1881; the first Democrat the state has elected to the Senate since John C. Stennis in 1982; and, per the Associated Press, the first Black person elected statewide in Mississippi since Reconstruction. He assumes office January 3, 2027.

The call is a projection until the vote is certified: county certification was due Friday, November 13, 2026, and the secretary of state's declaration is set for December 3, 2026. Mississippi has no automatic recount. Who succeeds subject as district attorney when he resigns that office is not yet in the open record pulled for this file.

ANALYST'S ASSESSMENT

The file turns on a single sheet of light-blue paper. In April 2023 one senator's quiet refusal ended a federal judgeship without a hearing, a vote, or a headline big enough to notice. Three years later the blocked nominee ran for the blocker's seat — in a state his party had not won a Senate race in since 1982, against forecasting that gave him less than a ten percent chance — and took it by half a percentage point, becoming Mississippi's first Black senator since Reconstruction. Whether the blue slip caused the run is subject's story to tell; the sequence is the public record's.

The prosecutor's file is double-sided in the same way. One half is the reformer's record: diversion, a first-in-the-state restorative justice program, a death-row exoneration. The other half is the file his opponents kept on him: the Soros-funded PAC money of 2015 and the prosecutors' letter of 2021 — the two items the blue-slip refusal itself cited. Both halves are documented; neither is invented. The desk records the whole folder.
