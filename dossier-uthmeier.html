BACKGROUND / COVER STORY

Subject is a Panhandle native who never held elected office until the state handed him one of its most powerful jobs — and then, twenty-one months later, asked the voters whether he could keep it. Raised in Destin, a Fort Walton Beach High School graduate whose parents had moved to the Panhandle from Missouri in the 1990s (Miami Herald profile, February 2026). Ran varsity track and cross-country at the University of Florida (bachelor's and master's degrees), then Georgetown University Law Center, class of 2014. Clerked around the U.S. Senate Judiciary Committee and for Marco Rubio while still in law school. No cover needed: every step of the file is a press release, a court record, or a campaign bio.

SERVICE RECORD (CAREER FILE)

  • 2014–2016 — Litigation associate, Jones Day, Washington, D.C. Campaign-side bios also credit him as counsel to Donald Trump's 2016 presidential campaign.
  • 2017–2019 — U.S. Department of Commerce, first Trump administration: senior advisor and counsel to Secretary Wilbur Ross. His portfolio included the administration's failed effort to add a citizenship question to the 2020 Census.
  • 2019–2020 — Returns to Florida: deputy general counsel to Governor Ron DeSantis, then general counsel — the governor's top in-house lawyer, managing litigation for the executive branch.
  • 2021–2025 — Chief of Staff to the Governor (October 2021), running the executive staff over some twenty agencies and more than one hundred thousand state employees (NAAG biography).
  • 2023–2024 — Doubles as campaign manager of DeSantis's unsuccessful 2024 presidential campaign.
  • Side posting — Adjunct professor, Florida State University College of Law (official biographies).
  • February 17, 2025 — Sworn in as the 39th Attorney General of Florida, appointed by Governor DeSantis to succeed Ashley Moody on her appointment to the U.S. Senate. At thirty-seven, one of the youngest state attorneys general in the country. He had never been elected to anything.

RECORD IN OFFICE (2025–2026)

  • Immigration enforcement, and a contempt citation. Subject directed state law enforcement to keep enforcing Florida's SB 4-C — which makes unauthorized entry into Florida a state crime — after a federal judge blocked the law. In June 2025, U.S. District Judge Kathleen M. Williams held him in contempt of court. Subject's position, on the record: he would not order agencies not party to the case to stand down, and if the judge chose to jail him over it, "I'm willing to take that."
  • "Alligator Alcatraz." Proposed and helped open the state-run immigration detention facility in the Everglades — the defining project of his first year. Opened July 1, 2025, with President Trump in attendance. The facility was shuttered in June 2026.
  • First-weeks offensives. A new office seal referencing the "Free State of Florida" (February 2025); a criminal investigation of Andrew and Tristan Tate (March 2025); subpoenas to Roblox over child safety, escalated to criminal subpoenas in October 2025 after investigations found predators using the platform to reach minors.
  • Investigations opened — JPMorgan Chase, over alleged "de-banking" of Trump Media in the shadow of the federal Arctic Frost investigation; a reopened state investigation into Raúl Castro's role in the 1996 Brothers to the Rescue shootdown (announced March 4, 2026); a multi-state subpoena to Dr. Anthony Fauci over alleged COVID-era "self-dealing"; a demand that The New York Times Co. produce six years of internal records.

THE HOPE FLORIDA FILE (DISPUTED — THE FULL RECORD, BOTH SIDES)

The mechanics, per the public record: in September 2024 Florida settled Medicaid overbilling claims against Centene Corp. for sixty-seven million dollars. Ten million of it went to the Hope Florida Foundation, a nonprofit associated with First Lady Casey DeSantis. The Foundation passed five million each to two nonprofits, Secure Florida's Future and Save Our Society from Drugs, which in turn donated eight and a half million to Keep Florida Clean — a political committee chaired by subject — which campaigned against Amendment 3, the 2024 recreational marijuana ballot measure.

State Representative Alex Andrade, who chaired the House's investigation, accused subject and the Foundation's outside counsel of "a conspiracy to commit money laundering and wire fraud." A Leon County grand jury report — sealed in January 2026, made public in August 2026 through litigation and reporting — found the ten million "misappropriated" and found that subject, then the governor's chief of staff, personally directed a recipient organization to apply for a Hope Florida grant and held authority over the funds' use. Subject's defense, on the record: the ten million was a voluntary "sweetener" Centene added on top of the fifty-seven million owed the state, paid to a charity, and was therefore "not state dollars, not Medicaid dollars"; he called the allegations a smear campaign, and Governor DeSantis agreed. In December 2025 the state's own required payment calculation to the federal government included the ten million in its Medicaid pass-through obligation, which critics said undercut that defense.

Then the courts ruled. On October 7, 2026, Florida's First District Court of Appeal ordered the complete expungement of the grand jury report: the grand jury had exceeded its legal authority by alleging criminal wrongdoing and censuring individuals without returning indictments; the court rejected the "misappropriation" conclusion itself, finding the cited statutes did not apply and that the money passed directly from Centene to the Foundation without ever becoming state funds; and the court held that the State Attorney's premature release of the report violated the statutory protections of the people named in it. The grand jury's legal conclusions stand invalidated. In September 2026, before that ruling, Florida House Democrats had pressed for a House investigation and impeachment inquiry. The voters rendered the final verdict in November (below).

CURRENT OPERATION — THE ELECTION

On November 3, 2026, subject faced the electorate for the first time in his life and won a full term as Attorney General, defeating former state senator José Javier Rodríguez, the Democratic nominee: 50.9 percent to 49.1 percent — 4,177,866 votes to 4,024,770 (unofficial totals; certification by the state Elections Canvassing Commission was still pending when this file was compiled). The margin, roughly one hundred fifty-three thousand votes, sits well outside Florida's half-percent automatic-recount threshold. Subject carried a ten-million-dollar fundraising advantage into the race; Democrats' national AG association spent multimillions trying to make Hope Florida the issue. It was not enough. His elected term runs through 2030.

ANALYST'S ASSESSMENT

The fastest ascent in Florida government: law-firm associate to state attorney general in about a decade, every promotion handed to him by one patron — until November 2026, when eight million voters got their say and ratified the appointment by the narrowest comfortable margin in the Cabinet. He went into that vote with the legal cloud formally lifted: the October expungement ruling threw out the grand jury report that had hung over his first term. His governing style in office is escalation as doctrine: enforce first, litigate second, accept a contempt citation as a credential rather than a wound. The open question the file cannot answer is whether the voters endorsed the man or simply the party — a Democrat has not won this office since Bob Butterworth in 1998, and subject's two-point margin ran behind his own ticket's history in the state. Watch the January term start: a man who governed as an appointee now holds a mandate of his own, and his list of open investigations suggests he intends to use it.
